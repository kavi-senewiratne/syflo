Citation card — six layouts for the same reference

Same shell throughout (FloatingPopup's rounded-2xl, gray-50 footer, blue-50 doors) and the same content: Bahdanau et al., 2014, 14,624 citations, CoRR. What changes is what the layout puts first.

The first round stacked the facts and varied the spacing — four versions of one idea. These six start from different questions instead: what does the reader actually need in the half-second before they decide?

Title, authors, done

Two facts decide it. Year, venue and count are one tap away for the rare moment they matter.

Reference [2]
Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate
Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, Yoshua Bengio
Why 6: a reader clicking a citation is asking one question — "do I want to read this?" — and the title plus the authors answer it. The rest is reference material, so it collapses into a single line that expands on demand. It is also the only layout whose height does not change between a fully resolved work and one read off the page.
1 is the runner-up and the most honest to the subject: it speaks the reader's own notation. It loses because a card is also a place to act, and a sentence gives the eye no anchor for the buttons. 2 makes citation count the headline — striking, but it ranks papers by popularity, which is not what a reference list is for. 5 tested worst: buttons above the title mean the reader acts before they have read anything.

The reference list — whole rows, with air under them

Underlining only the title was tried and rejected: with 40 entries the marks scattered, because a title sits somewhere different in every row. Whole rows give one steady rhythm — as long as the rule sits clear of the descenders.
